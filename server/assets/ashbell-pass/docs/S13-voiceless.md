# S13 — Voiceless

At noon on Day 4, Ves still cannot speak a single word.

She tries anyway. The bell tower is cold at this height, and the air smells of iron and warm stone. Every breath she draws goes past her cords and out again without a sound. She opens her mouth and shapes the start of Corin's name. What comes out is a dry hiss, like a kettle with the lid pressed down. She tries again, and a third time, until her eyes sting and her chest aches. Nothing changes. Above her the Ashbell hangs in the dark, its mouth still, and it looks exactly as it did last night. Somehow that is worse.

Through the open louvres, Kettlehold lies in the cold light like a handful of lamps someone dropped. Smoke rises from the Kettle & Coal and from the forges near Saltgate, and nobody in the street looks up. Once, the whole town would have looked up at this tower. Now people look at their feet, or at each other, and they keep walking.

Mother Brisk pressed a slate into her hands at breakfast, with a stub of chalk tied to it on a string. Ves carried it up the tower stair without thinking, the way she once carried her caravan ledger, and now she sits on the cold boards with the slate on her knee. Her fingers are numb. She writes in short, square letters: WHO HAS BEEN UP HERE? The chalk squeaks like a mouse in a wall. She looks at the words and does not like how angry they look, so she rubs them out with her sleeve and writes them again, smaller.

Near the lowest step there is a dark stain that someone has scrubbed and failed to lift completely. Corin fell on these stairs before dawn, and the town has talked about nothing else since. Ves looks at the stain for a long time before she looks away.

Through the Ashsight that the Ember Well gave her, the boards show faint warm prints, fading already, where someone climbed the stair in the night. She follows them with her eyes to the top step and then lets them go. She does not need the heat-trails to tell her who was here. She can hear him.

Footsteps sound below. Ves turns, chalk tight in her fist, but it is only one person, slow and uneven, one foot dragging on every step. Odo Fenn climbs up into the light from the louvres. He carries no lamp today, which is strange for a man who spends his nights lighting other people's way. He stops at the top of the stair. He looks at the bell, then at her, then at the slate, and he does not pretend he was passing by.

"Half this town calls me the Lamplighter," he says, "and the other half calls me worse. I can't help you talk, Ves. But you can listen, and then you can throw that slate at my head if you like."

She does not throw it. She sets the slate on her knee and waits. He lowers himself onto the step above her, one hand flat against the wall, and breathes for a while before he speaks again. Below them a cart rattles over the stones at the bottom of the tower, and a woman calls to someone about the price of oil.

"I owe Ysolde Marr more than a lamplighter earns in a lifetime." He says it plainly, the way he might read out a tally. "I have owed her for years. She came to me with a smile and a ledger, and she told me the bridge had to stay open for one night. She only wanted the Bronze Clapper out of the bell. I said yes. I took it down from the Ashbell myself."

Ves writes one word on the slate and holds it up. WHY. Odo looks at it for a long moment. "Because she paid, and I was behind on everything else," he says. "That is the whole of it, and it is not a good enough reason. I know that."

"I swore to Captain Rourke that I was on the Cinder Bridge that night, and I was not." He rubs his face with both hands. "I was in the Old Mine, under the gorge, with a lantern I was too ashamed to light."

Ves writes LIAR, looks at it, and rubs it out. She has known liars who were proud of it. Odo is not proud of anything he has told her this morning, and she recognises the look of a man who has stopped expecting to be forgiven.

Then there is a commotion at the foot of the stair. Four bearers come up the last turn with a chair between them, and Sister Agnet walks ahead, clearing the way with an impatient hand. Abbess Hesper Quill sits in the chair with a blanket over her knees and her eyes fixed on the top of the stair. It is the first time in years she has left the Abbey, and she makes sure everyone knows it is an inconvenience.

"You rang it, then," she says, dry as dust. "You rang it with your own throat and lost the only thing you came home with. That was not wise, child. It was not stupid either. Sit down, Odo, before you fall down."

Odo sits down. Ves lifts the slate and writes I RANG IT MYSELF. Hesper reads it without expression. "Ringing it was your own choice, and that matters more than the voice." She looks past Ves at the bell, and for a moment her face is not dry at all.

Captain Rourke Dale comes last, out of breath, without his cloak. He stands in the doorway and says nothing for a long moment. He looks older than Ves remembers, and his eyes are red at the rims. Then he says, "I wasn't watching the tower, Ves. I left my post to drink at the Kettle & Coal, and I'm ashamed of it."

Nobody in the tower says the obvious thing, which is that the bell will ring again at dusk and somebody will have to pay for it. Ves thinks about who that might be, and then she makes herself stop.

Hesper speaks of Ysolde last. "The town will hear Ysolde Marr at Saltgate," she says, "and decide what the broken oath and the broken bell will cost her." She glances at Ves. "You will be asked to speak for the bell when that happens, if you ever speak again." Hesper smiles at that, which Ves has never seen her do before.

Ves wipes the slate clean with the heel of her hand. Her fingers are steadier now. Maybe the voice is gone for another day, but the hand still knows how to hold chalk. She writes one more word and does not show it to anyone: NOT YET.
