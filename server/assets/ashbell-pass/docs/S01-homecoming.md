# S01 — Homecoming

The road climbs in long switchbacks, and the caravan goes up them one wagon at a time. Ves rides near the front on Old Daisy, her mule, who has an opinion about every slope and shares it loudly. Below the road the valley falls away into haze, and somewhere under the haze the steam vents hiss like kettles that someone has forgotten on the stove.

It is the afternoon of the first day. Ves left this pass six years ago with a scout's pack and a promise to write home. She wrote twice. Now the town she is climbing toward is the place she grew up in, and for the whole way up she has been trying not to think about what it will look like.

Then the trees thin out and she sees Kettlehold. Steam rises from a dozen vents along the ridge, white in the low sun, and the roofs sit among it like stones at the bottom of a pot of water. For a moment she cannot breathe properly. It is smaller than she remembered. It is also exactly the same.

Ves keeps her eyes on the road, because the road is what she is paid for. Her shoulders are tight under the strap of her pack, and she tells herself it is the climb.

At 16:00 on Day 1, Ves rides through the Bell Gate at the head of the Red Mule Caravan.

The Bell Gate is a squat tower of grey stone with iron bands around its base. The Pass Guard barracks sit against its flank like a cow leaning on a fence. Above the arch, the Ashbell hangs in its frame, dark and silent in the afternoon light, and Ves has to look up at it for a long moment before she can look anywhere else.

Sergeant Fennick comes out of the guardroom with a wax tablet under one arm. He checks every name in the caravan against his list, and he takes his time over Ves's, glancing up at her face twice before he makes his mark. "Scout, is it," he says. "They said you'd come back thinner." "They were wrong about that too," Ves says, and he almost smiles.

Ysolde Marr leans from her saddle and asks Fennick whether the Pass Guard has learned to count past three yet. He tells her the caravan is the only thing in the pass that has ever been late enough to notice. The guards laugh. Ysolde laughs loudest, tossing her braid back over one shoulder, and Ves notices that the laugh stops short of her eyes. Ysolde has always been good at that. It was the first thing Ves ever learned from her, and the last thing she is sure she understands.

Corin Ashdown is waiting for her on the tower stair. He stands with one hand on the rail and his coat buttoned to the throat. He is older than she remembers and greyer at the temples, and he looks at her the way a man looks at a debt he has already decided not to collect.

"Ves," he says. "You made good time."

"The road was long," she says.

He nods, as if she has told him something else entirely.

He taught her to read the signal fires from the tower and to keep her voice steady in a gale. He also taught her not to ask about his father, and she asked anyway, for years, until the questions wore out. Aldous Ashdown's name is still cut into the oldest beam of the stair, and Corin's shoulders go stiff whenever anyone reads it aloud.

Old Daisy plants her hooves at the foot of the stair and refuses to move until someone produces the last of the apples. A stable boy with cracked hands fetches one. Ves takes the rope from him, feeds the mule the apple herself, and says something low and private that makes Daisy's ears go flat and then forward again.

From the top step she can see the whole of Kiln Street. It runs straight down the hill from the Bell Gate toward Saltgate, lined on both sides with iron lamp posts, and the posts are dark. Ves stands with the wind at her back and looks down the length of it. She counts the lamps without meaning to, the way she used to as a girl, and when she reaches the bottom she starts again at the top.
