# Kettlehold and the Ashbell Pass

Kettlehold is the last town before the summit of the Ashbell Pass. Travellers who climb up from the plains see it first as a smear of white steam on the mountain face, and only later as roofs, chimneys and the grey line of the road. The town was built where the earth breathes. Warm vents run beneath its streets, and the Order's archivists say the first families came for the heat, not for the view.

## The shape of the pass

From the top down, the town is a set of steps. The Bell Gate is a tower of grey stone at the top of town, fortified and watchful, and the Pass Guard barracks stand beside it. Every cart that climbs the last hill passes under its arch.

Below the gate, Kiln Street runs downhill for the length of the town. It is lined with lamps that are lit at dusk and left to burn until the small hours. Halfway down, the street reaches Cinder Bridge, an iron span across the steam gorge. The bridge is warm to the hand in winter and slick with condensation in summer.

Beyond the bridge, the Abbey stands on its own shelf of rock above the gorge. Stillfire Abbey looks down on the town the way a watchman looks down on a gate.

Below the gorge, the Old Mine is cut into the slope. It is an ember-salt mine, and its mouth was closed with timber and iron. The Old Mine has been sealed since the Night of Ash.

At the bottom of the town is Saltgate, the market square by the old wall. The Kettle & Coal stands on the edge of Saltgate, so its back door opens straight onto the market stalls. Caravans come down to Saltgate to sell, and the inn is the first warm bed most of them find after the long climb.

The Foot of the Pass is the caravan camp half a day below the town, where teams rest their animals before the climb.

Caravans and locals use the same few roads, and each road has its own character. The climb from the Foot of the Pass is steep and open, and the wind comes down it hard in the afternoons. The lanes inside the town are narrow, crooked and roofed in places, so that a rider coming in from the high road often loses sight of the Bell Gate until the last bend. Most visitors find their way by listening for the bell and by following the lamps down Kiln Street.

## Living on steam

Houses are built over the vent pipes, so the floors stay warm through the winter and the snow on the roofs melts from below. Kilns along the lower wall burn in every season, and they are the reason the town has its glass, its tiles and its lamp oil. Gardens grow under glass panes set over warm ground, and in the coldest months the beds stay green while the snow lies deep on the paths.

When the vents run hot, the air smells of sulphur and the gorge fills with thick white steam. On those days the watch calls a steam day, and nobody crosses the bridge without a rope.

The high road itself has a name. Locals call the high road “the Kettle’s Spout” because steam rises from every crack in it. The name is older than the Pass Guard and is used by drivers and children alike.

## The dusk bell

Every dusk the Bellwarden rings the Ashbell. The sound carries down Kiln Street to Saltgate, across the gorge to the Abbey, and out along the road, and the people of Kettlehold stop what they are doing when it sounds. The bell warns of ashfall, the grey dust that the vents throw over the roofs on bad nights. It also marks the hour when the Cinder Bridge is closed for the night. No cart crosses the gorge after the bell.

Visitors who ask why the town keeps such a ritual are told that the bell has never missed a dusk. The price of ringing is not a thing the town discusses with visitors.

## Trade with the caravans

The caravans are the town's second pulse. The Red Mule Caravan carries salt and lamp oil up the pass, and it brings back whatever the lower valleys have to sell. Its wagons unload at Saltgate, its drivers drink at the inn, and its captains keep rooms along the lower part of Kiln Street. Every trader who stays the night sleeps within sound of the Bell Gate.

## Notes for designers

Kettlehold reads as a hub map. Every road from the Foot of the Pass runs up to the Bell Gate, and every path down from the Abbey crosses the Cinder Bridge. Keep Kiln Street as the spine of the town, so players always know which way is up. Mark the Kettle & Coal as the first safe stop after arrival, because the inn is where most players will find their first conversations. Use the vents as a visual cue: steam means the gorge is close, and a steam day means the bridge is dangerous.
