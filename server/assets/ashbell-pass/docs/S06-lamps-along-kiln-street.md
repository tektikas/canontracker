# S06 — Lamps Along Kiln Street

Night has settled over Kettlehold like a lid. Ves walks Kiln Street with her collar up, stopping at every doorway that still shows light. The lamps are lit now, one after another down the slope, and each one wears a small halo of steam that drifts sideways on the wind off the gorge. Ves asks her questions quietly. Who was out at dusk? Who went up the tower stair? Who has been drinking since the bell failed? Most people shake their heads or shut their doors.

A baker says he was asleep. A woman with a shawl says she heard nothing but the wind. One old man tells Ves that the bell has never failed in his lifetime, and he does not intend to live long enough to see it fail again. Ves thanks him and walks on. Each answer is a small stone, and none of them fits the gap she is trying to close.

The Kettle & Coal sits near the lower end of the street, its windows fogged at the edges and bright with firelight. Ves stops on the cold cobbles to look through the glass. At 21:30 on Day 2, Captain Rourke is drinking alone at the Kettle & Coal. He sits hunched at the end of the bar with his cap pushed back and a bottle at his elbow. No one sits beside him, and no one tries.

He will not thank her for interrupting his drink. Ves moves on.

The last lamps on Kiln Street give way to darkness where the street drops toward the gorge. Ves reaches the Cinder Bridge a short while later, at the place where it leaves the street. The iron arc stretches over the gorge into the dark, and the wind hums through its braces. Only one lantern burns, halfway across, and beside it a figure is standing with a long pole in both hands.

At 22:00 on Day 2, the Lamplighter is on the Cinder Bridge, trimming the last wick.

He turns as she approaches, and for a moment he looks like a man caught stealing bread. He is thin and stooped, and one leg does not bend properly, so he leans on the rail as he shifts his weight. Everyone on Kiln Street calls him the Lamplighter, and nobody who knows him uses anything else.

The Lamplighter keeps a tally of every lamp he has lit, scratched into the lining of his coat.

Ves asks him if he was on the bridge at dusk. He answers too quickly. "I was on the bridge," he says. "I always am. The lamps do not light themselves." He keeps his eyes on the lantern while he speaks. When he tries to tuck the wick trimmer back into his belt, his fingers miss the loop twice.

"Did you hear the bell?" Ves asks.

"I heard the wind," he says. "The wind was wrong tonight. You ask anyone." He says it the way a man says things when he wants to be left alone, and Ves does not push him. Not yet. She notes the nervous hands and the careful distance he keeps from the edge of the bridge, and she files them away.

When he finally moves off, he heads back along the bridge toward the street. At its town end he turns onto a set of stone steps that climb into the rock beside the cottages. The stair is steep and narrow, and it is not a stair anyone uses by choice after dark.

He climbs the Tallow Stair with his ladder over one shoulder.

Ves stands at the bottom and watches his small lantern rise through the shadows until it disappears behind a wall. The wind carries the smell of hot iron up from the gorge. Somewhere above her a shutter bangs, and a dog starts barking and stops. She turns back toward the lamps on Kiln Street, and she does not know yet which of the people she has spoken to tonight are telling her the truth.
