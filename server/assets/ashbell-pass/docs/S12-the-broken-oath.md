# S12 — The Broken Oath

The Bell Gate stairs are cold at the start of the day, and the steam from the vents hangs low over the steps like wet wool. Ves sits halfway down with a slate on her knees. Her voice is gone, and her throat feels scraped raw and too tight to swallow. The town below is still waking, and the smell of bread drifts up from the bakeries on the lower street.

At 08:00 on Day 4, Ysolde's sworn partner, Corin, meets her at the foot of the Bell Gate stairs. He comes down the steps slowly, one hand on the rail, and he does not hurry for anyone. Ysolde waits for him at the bottom with her hat in her hands, as if she is the one who came to beg.

Corin does not shout. He stands very still, the splinted arm held against his ribs, and he waits for her to finish. His face is the colour of old ash. Ves can see that he has not slept, and that the pain in his arm is the least of what keeps him awake.

Ves writes on a slate with a stub of chalk, pressing so hard that the chalk squeaks. She has no voice to ask the question, so she asks it in capitals. She turns the slate so Corin can read it, and his jaw tightens. The question is short, and it is the only one that matters on the stairs.

Ysolde admits she paid Odo Fenn to take the Bronze Clapper. She says it without looking away, the way she names prices in the market. There is no shame in her voice and no apology, only the flat tone of someone reading out a bill that she has already paid in full.

Ves watches her hands while she talks. They are still, the rings turned inward, the way they were when Ysolde made promises on the caravan road. Ves remembers that stillness from years ago, and it has never once meant that Ysolde was telling the truth.

She wanted the Cinder Bridge open after dusk for one run, and she wanted it without asking. Corin had said no, and she had gone around him anyway. Ves can hear the old argument underneath the new one, the one about the caravan and the salt and who would pay for it.

Her coin went to Odo, and Odo did the rest. The clapper was taken from the tower while the bell hung silent above the town, and the whole town felt the silence afterwards. Ysolde does not say how Odo managed the stair. She does not need to. Everyone on the Bell Gate stairs has seen what a debt can make a man do.

She did it, she says, for the caravan, and for the salt that feeds the lower town through winter. She says it as if it were a reason, and for her it may well be. Corin does not answer. He has heard that kind of reasoning from her before, usually just before she asked him for something he could not give.

Some of the stallholders have begun to gather at the bottom of the lower steps, pretending to haggle over bread while they listen. Corin notices them and says nothing. Ves can almost hear what they are thinking: that the Bell Gate has finally broken, and that an oath was never much more than a word spoken in the wind.

Corin sits down on the lowest step of the Bell Gate stairs without being asked. He does not raise his voice at her. He does not need to. Ves watches the anger settle into his face slowly, like frost creeping across a window, and she understands that this is the worst thing he has ever shown her.

Ves turns the slate back toward herself and writes one more line, then holds it up for Ysolde to read. Ysolde reads it and does not reply. The steam keeps rising from the vents, and the three of them stand on the stairs in the thin morning light, with the bell silent overhead and a broken oath between them.
