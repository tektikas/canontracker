# S04 — Embers and Oaths

Late morning on Day 2, and the Bell Gate is already warm. Steam seeps up between the paving stones and curls around the base of the tower. Ves stands in its thin shade with a cup of tea going cold in her hand. She came up Kiln Street at first light behind the last mule of the Red Mule Caravan. Six years on the road still sit in her shoulders, and the town still smells of sulphur and fresh bread, the way it did when she was a girl.

Corin is at the foot of the tower, where he always stands before the bell is rung. Ysolde Marr finds him there. She moves with the easy stride of a woman who has never once been made to wait, and she lays a hand on his sleeve as if the two of them were still partners. Corin looks at the hand until she takes it back.

Ysolde asks Corin to leave the Cinder Bridge open after dusk, just once.

"It is one night," Ysolde says. "One late run, one cart, and the gorge will not swallow a single sack of salt." Her voice is light, but Ves knows that tone. It is the one Ysolde used years ago when she sold a lame horse to a farmer and smiled while he counted out the coins.

"The bridge closes when the bell rings," Corin says. "It has closed every dusk since my father stood where I am standing. You know why it closes."

He does not look up at the tower, and he does not need to. Every child on Kiln Street knows the story of the Night of Ash: the lower town buried under grey dust, and the man who kept ringing the Ashbell until the ash took him. Ves learned it as a child, from every adult who ever told her to stay off the bridge.

"I am asking for one night, Corin."

"I know exactly what you are asking."

Ves watches them from the edge of the shade. They keep their voices down, so the words carry only so far. Two guards by the gate pretend not to listen. A salt trader hauling sacks up the steps stops, sees who is talking, and keeps climbing. Ves cannot hear everything, but she catches the important words: law, ash, and "father," which Corin says as if it costs him something to say it aloud.

He refuses. He does it the way he refuses everything, with his jaw set and his hands held open, as though he is offering something he has no right to keep. The law is the law. His father bled for that bell. The ash is still down in the gorge, and the bridge is the only thing standing between the town and the drop. Ysolde listens with her head tilted. When he finishes, she smiles as if he has told her a joke she has heard many times before.

"Hesper would laugh at the pair of us," she says. "She has been laughing at this gate for ninety years."

Then Ysolde notices Ves, and her face opens into a warm grin. "Scout!" she calls. "Look at you. Six years and you still stand like someone waiting for a rockfall." Ves nods and says nothing useful. Corin turns his back and walks to the tower door, and the iron door closes behind him with a dull knock.

"Walk with me," Ysolde says, and hooks her arm through Ves's before she can refuse. They go down Kiln Street together. The lamps along the street are still unlit, their glass dull in the sun. Ysolde asks about the road, about Old Daisy's temper on the switchbacks, about the wet spring in the high passes. When Ves asks what is in the wagons, Ysolde talks about the price of lamp oil instead. She laughs at the right moments and squeezes Ves's arm at the wrong ones.

Near Saltgate she sends a small boy running with a coin and a message she does not share. Ves watches him go. She does not ask where the message is headed. She suspects Ysolde would only lie, and she would rather hear the lie than a truth delivered without warmth.

The light turns amber over the gorge. Ysolde stops at the lower end of the street, where the market stalls are folding up, and glances back toward the Bell Gate. The tower stands dark against the sky, and for a moment she looks like someone counting. Then she turns, climbs back up the hill with her hands buried in her coat, and does not look round.
